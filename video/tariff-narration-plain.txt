This video explains how a tariff diagram works, using the market for steel.

Here we have the domestic market for steel: an upward-sloping supply curve and a downward-sloping demand curve.

Now we bring in the world supply of steel, at a price of two hundred dollars a tonne. It's drawn as a perfectly elastic — that is, horizontal — supply curve, because we're a small open economy: the world price is fixed at two hundred dollars, and nothing we do here can move it.

At that world price, our domestic producers are willing to supply twenty thousand tonnes.

Buyers want more than that, and the rest is met by imports. We measure imports as the horizontal distance between the supply and demand curves — here, sixty thousand tonnes.

Domestic production plus imports gives us total demand: eighty thousand tonnes.

Now we can read some revenues straight off the diagram, and revenue is simply price times quantity. Domestic producer revenue is the world price times the quantity our own firms supply — two hundred dollars times twenty thousand.

Foreign producer revenue is the world price times the quantity imported — two hundred dollars times sixty thousand.

And total consumer spending is the price times the total quantity bought — two hundred dollars times eighty thousand.

Now we introduce a tariff, which is simply a tax on imports.

We measure the size of the tariff as the vertical distance between the world price and the new price with the tariff. Here we've lifted it by two hundred dollars, so the tariff is two hundred dollars a tonne — and the price of steel in our economy rises to four hundred dollars.

That higher price expands domestic production: our producers move up their supply curve, from twenty thousand to forty thousand tonnes.

Imports fall, down to twenty thousand tonnes, and they fall for two reasons: domestic production has risen, and, at the higher price, demand has contracted.

That contraction in demand takes total demand from eighty thousand down to sixty thousand tonnes.

Now the revenues again, at the new price. Domestic producer revenue is four hundred dollars times forty thousand tonnes.

Government tariff revenue is the tariff itself, two hundred dollars, times the quantity of imports, twenty thousand tonnes. On the diagram, it is the rectangle sitting directly on top of the world price, between the world price and the tariff price, across the imported quantity.

Foreign producer revenue is the world price times the imports: two hundred dollars times twenty thousand tonnes. Here is the important bit. Steel now sells for four hundred dollars, but foreign producers still only get two hundred. That is because the importer who brings the steel in pays the two-hundred-dollar tariff to the government. So the foreign producer keeps the world price, which is the lower rectangle, and the government takes the tariff, the rectangle stacked on top.

And total consumer spending is the new price times the total quantity: four hundred dollars times sixty thousand tonnes.

So, in summary. Consumers pay a higher price. There is increased domestic production and revenue, which can mean more employment in these protected industries. The higher price causes a contraction in demand. The government collects tariff revenue. And foreign producers lose out, with falling revenue and fewer imports coming into the economy.

In short: a tariff lifts the price, protects domestic firms and jobs, contracts demand, raises revenue for the government, and cuts foreign sales.
