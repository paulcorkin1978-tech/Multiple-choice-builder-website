## The economic problem

Every society faces the same basic problem: our wants are unlimited, but the resources we have to satisfy them are scarce. We can't have everything we want, so we're forced to choose. And every choice has a cost — not the money we hand over, but the next-best alternative we give up. That's opportunity cost, and it sits underneath almost everything we study this year.

Think about it in three questions. What to produce? How to produce it? And who gets it once it's produced? Different economic systems answer those questions in different ways. In a pure market economy, prices and private decisions do the deciding. In a planned economy, the government does. Australia, like most countries, sits somewhere in between — a mixed economy, where markets do most of the work but the government steps in where it thinks it needs to.

## How markets work

A market is simply any arrangement where buyers and sellers come together and a price gets set. The demand curve slopes downward, because as something gets cheaper, people are willing and able to buy more of it. The supply curve slopes upward, because as the price rises, producers find it worth their while to supply more. Where the two curves cross, we get the equilibrium — the price where the quantity buyers want exactly matches the quantity sellers offer.

What's clever about this is that nobody plans it. If the price is too high, there's a surplus, unsold stock piles up, and sellers cut their prices. If the price is too low, there's a shortage, buyers compete, and the price gets bid up. The market clears itself. Prices act as signals, telling producers what to make and consumers what they can afford.

## Elasticity

Elasticity measures how responsive we are to a change in price. If the price of something jumps and people barely change how much they buy, we call demand inelastic — think petrol, or bread, or anything we treat as a necessity. If a small price rise sends people running to alternatives, demand is elastic — think one particular brand of soft drink when three others sit beside it on the shelf.

What makes demand elastic? A few things: whether there are close substitutes, whether the item is a luxury or a necessity, and how big a slice of our income it takes up. It matters in the real world, too. When a government puts a tax on cigarettes to cut smoking, it's relying on demand being at least somewhat elastic. And when a business decides whether to run a sale, it's really asking a question about elasticity: will the extra sales more than make up for the lower price?

## Economic growth and the business cycle

Economic growth means an increase in the volume of goods and services an economy produces over time. We usually measure it through Gross Domestic Product — GDP — the total value of everything produced within a country in a year. When GDP is rising, the economy is growing; incomes tend to rise, and unemployment tends to fall.

But growth isn't smooth. Economies move through a business cycle — periods of expansion, then a peak, then a downturn or contraction, then a trough, and then recovery. In a boom, spending is strong, businesses hire, and prices can start to climb. In a downturn, spending falls, firms cut back, and unemployment rises. A big part of economic management is trying to smooth out that cycle — to keep growth steady rather than lurching from boom to bust.

## Unemployment

Someone is unemployed if they're willing and able to work, and actively looking, but can't find a job. We measure it as a percentage of the labour force. But the headline number hides a lot. There's cyclical unemployment, which rises and falls with the business cycle. There's structural unemployment, where people's skills no longer match the jobs available — often after an industry declines or technology changes. There's frictional unemployment, the normal churn of people moving between jobs. And there's seasonal unemployment, tied to the time of year.

The costs are real, and they're not only economic. For the economy, unemployment means lost output — goods and services we could have produced but didn't. For the individual and their family, it can mean lost income, lost skills, and a real toll on wellbeing. That's why keeping unemployment low is one of the central goals of economic policy.

## Inflation

Inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level over time. A little inflation is normal, and even healthy. Too much is a problem, because it erodes the value of money — the same twenty dollars buys less this year than it did last year. It hurts people on fixed incomes especially hard, and it makes it difficult for businesses to plan.

Where does it come from? Sometimes it's demand-pull inflation: spending in the economy runs ahead of what the economy can produce, and prices get bid up. Sometimes it's cost-push inflation: the costs of production rise — wages, or imported materials, or energy — and businesses pass those higher costs on. Managing inflation is largely the job of the central bank, and that brings us to policy.

## Fiscal policy

Fiscal policy is the use of the government's budget — its spending and its taxation — to influence the economy. If the economy is weak and unemployment is rising, the government can run an expansionary policy: spend more, or tax less, putting money into people's hands and lifting demand. If the economy is overheating and inflation is climbing, it can do the opposite — a contractionary stance, spending less or taxing more to take some heat out.

The budget outcome tells the story. A deficit, where the government spends more than it raises, tends to add demand to the economy. A surplus, where it raises more than it spends, tends to subtract from it. And a balanced budget sits in between. Fiscal policy is powerful, but it's not always quick — decisions take time to legislate and even longer to have their full effect.

## Monetary policy

Monetary policy is run by the central bank, and in Australia that's the Reserve Bank. Its main tool is the cash rate — the interest rate that flows through to the rates banks charge on loans and pay on savings. The logic is straightforward. If the Reserve Bank wants to slow the economy and bring inflation down, it raises the cash rate. Borrowing gets more expensive, people and businesses spend less, and demand eases. If it wants to support a weak economy, it cuts the rate, borrowing gets cheaper, and spending is encouraged.

Compared with fiscal policy, monetary policy is more flexible — the Reserve Bank meets regularly and can move quickly. But it's a blunt instrument. An interest rate change affects the whole economy at once; it can't target one struggling region or one industry. That's why the two policies are usually used together.

## Globalisation and trade

Globalisation is the growing integration of economies around the world — through trade, through investment, through the movement of people, and through technology. Countries trade because it makes them better off. The principle of comparative advantage says that even if one country could produce everything more efficiently, both countries still gain if each specialises in what it's relatively best at and trades for the rest.

Free trade lowers prices for consumers and widens the range of goods available. But it also exposes domestic industries to foreign competition, and that's where governments sometimes intervene — with tariffs, subsidies, and quotas, the very tools we've been drawing on the graph. Each one protects domestic producers in a different way, and each one has a different cost, whether that falls on consumers, on taxpayers, or on our trading relationships. The job of an economist isn't to say protection is always good or always bad — it's to trace through exactly who gains, who loses, and by how much.

## Bringing it together

That's really the heart of this course. We're not just learning definitions; we're learning to think through consequences. When a price changes, who responds, and by how much? When the government spends or taxes or lifts a rate, what ripples out from that decision? Economics gives us a way to reason about those questions carefully — to look past the headline and ask what's actually going on underneath. Keep that habit of mind, and the rest of the syllabus falls into place.
