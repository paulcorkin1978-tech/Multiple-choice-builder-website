# Explainer voiceover scripts — subsidy, tariff, quota

Read each line as one slide, then click to the next. Speak the way you want the videos to sound — the ElevenLabs clone copies your delivery, so this recording is both your draft voiceover *and* your training audio.

**Before you hit record**

- Quiet room, door shut, phone silent. Record the whole lot in one sitting so the tone stays consistent.
- Mic ~15–20 cm away, angled slightly across your mouth (not straight on) to avoid pops.
- No noise reduction, compression, EQ or effects afterward — ElevenLabs wants it raw. Export WAV.
- Aim for a comfortable, steady level (roughly −23 to −18 dB average, peaks under −3 dB). If you fluff a line, pause a beat and re-read it — you can trim later.
- Rough runtime of all three is ~12–15 minutes, so you'll be short of the 30-minute minimum. Top up afterward by reading a few pages of your own teaching notes in the same voice.

## 1 · Subsidy — the market for beef  (24 slides, 0–23)

**0.** Let's look at how a subsidy works — using the market for beef.

**1.** Here's the domestic supply and demand for beef.

**2.** Australia's a small player in the world beef market, so we take the world price as given — six dollars a kilo.

**3.** At six dollars, our own farmers are willing to produce sixty thousand tonnes.

**4.** But buyers want a hundred and forty thousand tonnes. That eighty-thousand-tonne gap is filled by imports.

**5.** Put those together and you've got total demand — a hundred and forty thousand tonnes.

**6.** Now the revenue. Domestic producers' sales revenue is price times quantity — six dollars times sixty thousand tonnes.

**7.** Foreign producers earn the same way — six dollars across the eighty thousand tonnes they supply.

**8.** And total consumer spending is the price times everything bought — six dollars times a hundred and forty thousand.

**9.** Now the government steps in with a four-dollar subsidy. Because farmers collect four dollars from the government, they need four dollars less from the market — so the supply curve slides down by four dollars.

**10.** That vertical gap between the two supply curves is the subsidy — four dollars a kilo, at every quantity.

**11.** The market price is still six dollars — but on the new supply curve, farmers now produce a hundred thousand tonnes.

**12.** Domestic production has grown, so imports get pushed out — from eighty thousand tonnes down to forty thousand.

**13.** Total demand hasn't changed, though — buyers still want a hundred and forty thousand tonnes at six dollars.

**14.** Let's build domestic producer revenue. First, their sales revenue from buyers — six dollars times a hundred thousand tonnes, six hundred thousand dollars.

**15.** On top of that, the government pays the subsidy — four dollars times a hundred thousand tonnes, four hundred thousand dollars.

**16.** Add them together and total domestic producer revenue is one million dollars — six hundred thousand from buyers, four hundred thousand from the taxpayer.

**17.** Foreign producers now earn less — six dollars across just forty thousand tonnes, two hundred and forty thousand dollars.

**18.** And consumers? They still pay six dollars for the same hundred and forty thousand tonnes — their spending is completely unchanged.

**19.** So, the effects. One — increased domestic production.

**20.** Two — a reduction in imports.

**21.** Three — consumers pay the same price for the same beef.

**22.** And four — the taxpayer foots the bill: four hundred thousand dollars.

**23.** In short: a subsidy props up domestic producers and their workers, squeezes out foreign competitors, leaves households paying exactly the same — and it's funded by government spending.

## 2 · Tariff — the market for steel  (23 slides, 0–22)

**0.** This video explains how a tariff diagram works — using the market for steel.

**1.** Here we have the domestic market for steel: an upward-sloping supply curve and a downward-sloping demand curve.

**2.** Now we bring in the world supply of steel, at a price of two hundred dollars a tonne. It's drawn as a perfectly elastic — that is, horizontal — supply curve, because we're a small open economy: the world price is fixed at two hundred dollars, and nothing we do here can move it.

**3.** At that world price, our domestic producers are willing to supply twenty thousand tonnes.

**4.** Buyers want more than that, and the rest is met by imports. We measure imports as the horizontal distance between the supply and demand curves — here, sixty thousand tonnes.

**5.** Domestic production plus imports gives us total demand — eighty thousand tonnes.

**6.** Now we can read some revenues straight off the diagram, and revenue is simply price times quantity. Domestic producer revenue is the world price times the quantity our own firms supply — two hundred dollars times twenty thousand.

**7.** Foreign producer revenue is the world price times the quantity imported — two hundred dollars times sixty thousand.

**8.** And total consumer spending is the price times the total quantity bought — two hundred dollars times eighty thousand.

**9.** Now we introduce a tariff — which is simply a tax on imports.

**10.** We measure the size of the tariff as the vertical distance between the world price and the new price with the tariff. Here we've lifted it by two hundred dollars, so the tariff is two hundred dollars a tonne — and the price of steel in our economy rises to four hundred dollars.

**11.** That higher price expands domestic production: our producers move up their supply curve, from twenty thousand to forty thousand tonnes.

**12.** Imports fall — down to twenty thousand tonnes — and they fall for two reasons: domestic production has risen, and, at the higher price, demand has contracted.

**13.** That contraction in demand takes total demand from eighty thousand down to sixty thousand tonnes.

**14.** Now the revenues again, at the new price. Domestic producer revenue is four hundred dollars times forty thousand tonnes.

**15.** Government tariff revenue is the tariff itself — two hundred dollars — times the quantity of imports, twenty thousand tonnes. On the diagram it's the rectangle sitting directly on top of the world price, between the world price and the tariff price, across the imported quantity.

**16.** Foreign producer revenue is the world price times the imports — two hundred dollars times twenty thousand tonnes. Here's the important bit: steel now sells for four hundred dollars, but foreign producers still only get two hundred. That's because the importer who brings the steel in pays the two-hundred-dollar tariff to the government. So the foreign producer keeps the world price — that's the lower rectangle — and the government takes the tariff, the rectangle stacked on top.

**17.** And total consumer spending is the new price times the total quantity — four hundred dollars times sixty thousand tonnes.

**18.** So, in summary. One — consumers pay a higher price. And two — there's increased domestic production and revenue, which can mean more employment in these protected industries.

**19.** Three — that higher price causes a contraction in demand.

**20.** Four — the government collects tariff revenue.

**21.** And five — foreign producers lose out: falling revenue, and fewer imports coming into the economy.

**22.** In short: a tariff lifts the price, protects domestic firms and jobs, contracts demand, raises revenue for the government — and cuts foreign sales.

## 3 · Quota — the market for cars  (23 slides, 0–22)

**0.** Finally, let's look at a quota — using the market for cars.

**1.** Here's domestic supply and domestic demand for cars.

**2.** And the world price — forty thousand dollars a car.

**3.** At forty thousand, domestic makers produce a hundred thousand cars.

**4.** Buyers want four hundred thousand, so the three-hundred-thousand gap is imported.

**5.** Together, that's total demand — four hundred thousand cars.

**6.** Domestic producer revenue is price times quantity — forty thousand dollars times a hundred thousand cars. That's four billion dollars.

**7.** Foreign producers earn forty thousand across their three hundred thousand cars — twelve billion dollars.

**8.** And total consumer spending — forty thousand times all four hundred thousand cars, sixteen billion.

**9.** Now a quota is imposed. Unlike a tariff, it isn't a price — it's a hard numerical limit on imports. Watch the import gap between supply and demand get squeezed down to just two hundred thousand cars.

**10.** With imports restricted, the price is bid up, and domestic makers move up their supply curve — production rises to a hundred and fifty thousand cars.

**11.** Buyers move up the demand curve, so total demand falls to three hundred and fifty thousand.

**12.** The imports filling the gap between them are exactly the two hundred thousand the quota allows — and no more.

**13.** Together, that's total demand — three hundred and fifty thousand cars.

**14.** Domestic producer revenue — now at the higher price, sixty thousand dollars times a hundred and fifty thousand cars. Nine billion dollars.

**15.** Foreign producers sell fewer cars, but at that higher quota price — sixty thousand times two hundred thousand. Still twelve billion dollars.

**16.** And total consumer spending — sixty thousand times three hundred and fifty thousand cars, twenty-one billion.

**17.** So the effects. One — a higher price. And two — increased domestic production and revenue.

**18.** Three — a contraction in demand.

**19.** Four — a reduction in imports.

**20.** Five — and this is the key one: both domestic and foreign producers get to sell at the higher price.

**21.** Six — but unlike a tariff, the government collects no revenue, and spends nothing either.

**22.** In short: a quota lifts the price and protects domestic firms and jobs, foreign producers sell less but at a higher price — and the government neither earns nor spends a cent.
