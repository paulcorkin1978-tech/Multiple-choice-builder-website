# Explainer voiceover scripts — subsidy, tariff, quota

Read each line as one slide, then click to the next. Speak the way you want the videos to sound — the ElevenLabs clone copies your delivery, so this recording is both your draft voiceover *and* your training audio.

**Before you hit record**

- Quiet room, door shut, phone silent. Record the whole lot in one sitting so the tone stays consistent.
- Mic ~15–20 cm away, angled slightly across your mouth (not straight on) to avoid pops.
- No noise reduction, compression, EQ or effects afterward — ElevenLabs wants it raw. Export WAV.
- Aim for a comfortable, steady level (roughly −23 to −18 dB average, peaks under −3 dB). If you fluff a line, pause a beat and re-read it — you can trim later.
- Rough runtime of all three is ~12–15 minutes, so you'll be short of the 30-minute minimum. Top up afterward by reading a few pages of your own teaching notes in the same voice.

## 1 · Subsidy — the market for beef  (24 slides, 0–23)

**0.** Let's look at how a subsidy works — using the market for beef.

**1.** Here's the domestic supply and demand for beef.

**2.** Australia's a small player in the world beef market, so we take the world price as given — six dollars a kilo.

**3.** At six dollars, our own farmers are willing to produce sixty tonnes.

**4.** Buyers want a hundred and forty tonnes in total — that's total demand.

**5.** Our farmers supply sixty, so the remaining eighty tonnes are imported.

**6.** Now the revenue, and revenue is just price times quantity. Start with total consumer spending — six dollars times a hundred and forty, eight hundred and forty dollars.

**7.** Of that, domestic producers' sales revenue — six dollars times sixty, three hundred and sixty dollars.

**8.** And foreign producer revenue — six dollars times eighty, four hundred and eighty dollars.

**9.** Now the government steps in with a four-dollar subsidy. Because farmers collect four dollars from the government, they need four dollars less from the market — so the supply curve slides down by four dollars.

**10.** That vertical gap between the two supply curves is the subsidy — four dollars a kilo, at every quantity.

**11.** The market price is still six dollars — but on the new supply curve, farmers now produce a hundred tonnes.

**12.** Total demand hasn't changed — buyers still want a hundred and forty tonnes at six dollars.

**13.** So imports get pushed out — from eighty tonnes down to forty.

**14.** The revenues again. Total consumer spending is still six dollars times a hundred and forty — eight hundred and forty dollars, so consumers pay the same.

**15.** Of that, domestic producers' sales revenue from buyers — six dollars times a hundred, six hundred dollars.

**16.** And foreign producer revenue — six dollars times forty, two hundred and forty dollars.

**17.** On top of that, the government pays the subsidy — four dollars times a hundred, four hundred dollars.

**18.** Add the sales and the subsidy together, and total domestic producer revenue is a thousand dollars — six hundred from buyers, four hundred from the taxpayer.

**19.** So, the effects. One — increased domestic production.

**20.** Two — a reduction in imports.

**21.** Three — consumers pay the same price for the same beef.

**22.** And four — the taxpayer foots the bill: four hundred dollars.

**23.** In short: a subsidy props up domestic producers and their workers, squeezes out foreign competitors, leaves households paying exactly the same — and it's funded by government spending.

## 2 · Tariff — the market for steel  (23 slides, 0–22)

**0.** This video explains how a tariff diagram works — using the market for steel.

**1.** Here we have the domestic market for steel: an upward-sloping supply curve and a downward-sloping demand curve.

**2.** Now we bring in the world supply of steel, at a price of two hundred dollars a tonne. It's drawn as a perfectly elastic — that is, horizontal — supply curve, because we're a small open economy: the world price is fixed at two hundred dollars, and nothing we do here can move it.

**3.** At that world price, our domestic producers are willing to supply twenty tonnes.

**4.** At the world price, buyers want eighty tonnes in total — that is total demand.

**5.** Our domestic mills supply twenty, so the remaining sixty are met by imports — the horizontal gap between the supply and demand curves.

**6.** Now we can read some revenues straight off the diagram, and revenue is simply price times quantity. Start with total consumer spending — the price times the total quantity bought, two hundred dollars times eighty.

**7.** Of that spending, a proportion is domestic producer revenue — the world price times what our own firms supply, two hundred dollars times twenty.

**8.** And the remaining proportion is foreign producer revenue — the world price times the quantity imported, two hundred dollars times sixty.

**9.** Now we introduce a tariff — which is simply a tax on imports.

**10.** We measure the size of the tariff as the vertical distance between the world price and the new price with the tariff. Here we've lifted it by two hundred dollars, so the tariff is two hundred dollars a tonne — and the price of steel in our economy rises to four hundred dollars.

**11.** That higher price expands domestic production: our producers move up their supply curve, from twenty to forty tonnes.

**12.** Imports fall — down to twenty tonnes — and they fall for two reasons: domestic production has risen, and, at the higher price, demand has contracted.

**13.** That contraction in demand takes total demand from eighty down to sixty tonnes.

**14.** Now the revenues again, at the new price. Start with total consumer spending — the new price times the total quantity, four hundred dollars times sixty tonnes.

**15.** Of that, domestic producer revenue is four hundred dollars times the forty tonnes our own firms now supply.

**16.** Government tariff revenue is the tariff itself — two hundred dollars — times the twenty tonnes imported. On the diagram it's the rectangle sitting directly on top of the world price, between the world price and the tariff price, across the imports.

**17.** And foreign producer revenue is the world price times the imports — two hundred dollars times twenty tonnes. Here's the important bit: steel now sells for four hundred dollars, but foreign producers still only get two hundred. That's because the importer who brings the steel in pays the two-hundred-dollar tariff to the government. So the foreign producer keeps the world price — that's the lower rectangle — and the government takes the tariff, the rectangle stacked on top.

**18.** So, in summary. One — consumers pay a higher price. And two — there's increased domestic production and revenue, which can mean more employment in these protected industries.

**19.** Three — that higher price causes a contraction in demand.

**20.** Four — the government collects tariff revenue.

**21.** And five — foreign producers lose out: falling revenue, and fewer imports coming into the economy.

**22.** In short: a tariff lifts the price, protects domestic firms and jobs, contracts demand, raises revenue for the government — and cuts foreign sales.

## 3 · Quota — the market for cars  (22 slides, 0–21)

**0.** Finally, let's look at a quota — using the market for cars.

**1.** Here's domestic supply and domestic demand for cars.

**2.** And the world price — forty thousand dollars a car.

**3.** At forty thousand, domestic makers produce a hundred cars.

**4.** Buyers want four hundred cars in total — that's total demand.

**5.** Domestic makers supply a hundred, so the remaining three hundred are imported.

**6.** Now the revenue, and revenue is price times quantity. Start with total consumer spending — forty thousand times four hundred cars, sixteen million dollars.

**7.** Of that, domestic producer revenue — forty thousand times a hundred, four million dollars.

**8.** And foreign producer revenue — forty thousand times three hundred, twelve million dollars.

**9.** Now a quota is imposed. Unlike a tariff, it isn't a price — it's a hard numerical limit on imports. Watch the import gap between supply and demand get squeezed down to just two hundred cars.

**10.** With imports restricted, the price is bid up, and domestic makers move up their supply curve — production rises to a hundred and fifty cars.

**11.** Buyers move up the demand curve, so total demand falls to three hundred and fifty cars.

**12.** The imports filling that gap are exactly the two hundred the quota allows — and no more.

**13.** Now the revenues again. Total consumer spending is sixty thousand times three hundred and fifty cars — twenty-one million dollars.

**14.** Of that, domestic producer revenue — sixty thousand times a hundred and fifty, nine million dollars.

**15.** And foreign producer revenue — the quota price, sixty thousand, times two hundred: still twelve million dollars. They sell fewer cars, but at the higher price.

**16.** So the effects. One — a higher price. And two — increased domestic production and revenue.

**17.** Three — a contraction in demand.

**18.** Four — a reduction in imports.

**19.** Five — and this is the key one: both domestic and foreign producers get to sell at the higher price.

**20.** Six — but unlike a tariff, the government collects no revenue, and spends nothing either.

**21.** In short: a quota lifts the price and protects domestic firms and jobs, foreign producers sell less but at a higher price — and the government neither earns nor spends a cent.

**22.** In short: a quota lifts the price and protects domestic firms and jobs, foreign producers sell less but at a higher price — and the government neither earns nor spends a cent.
